Compound Interest Formula

Compound interest is calculated using the following formula for the future value of an investment with regular contributions:

Compound Interest Formula
A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt) + PMT x [((1 + r/n)^(nt) - 1) / (r/n)]

Where A is the future value, P is the initial principal, r is the annual interest rate (decimal), n is the number of compounding periods per year, t is the number of years, and PMT is the regular contribution amount.

How to Use This Calculator

  1. Enter your initial deposit - the lump sum you start with.
  2. Enter your monthly contribution - the amount you add each month (can be $0).
  3. Enter the annual interest rate - the expected annual return rate.
  4. Enter the number of years - your investment time horizon.
  5. Choose a compounding frequency - how often interest is calculated and added.
  6. Click "Calculate" to see your projected future value.

Example Scenario

If you invest $5,000 initially and add $200 per month at a 7% annual return compounded monthly for 20 years, your investment would grow to approximately $109,466. Of that, $53,000 would be your contributions and $56,466 would be interest earned - demonstrating the incredible power of compound interest over time.

Frequently Asked Questions

Compound interest is interest calculated on both the initial principal and the accumulated interest from previous periods. This creates a snowball effect where your money grows exponentially over time, often called "interest on interest."

More frequent compounding (daily vs. annually) means interest is calculated and added to your balance more often, resulting in slightly higher returns. The difference is most noticeable with larger balances and higher interest rates.

The Rule of 72 is a quick way to estimate how long it takes to double your money. Divide 72 by the annual interest rate to get the approximate number of years. For example, at 6% interest, your money doubles in about 12 years (72 / 6 = 12).

Regular contributions amplify compound interest because each new deposit starts earning interest immediately. Over decades, even small monthly contributions can result in hundreds of thousands of dollars due to the compounding effect.

Yes, compound interest also applies to debt like credit cards and some loans. Unpaid interest gets added to your balance, and you end up paying interest on interest, which can cause debt to grow rapidly if not managed.
