ETF Return Formula

The gross value (before expense ratio) and the net value (after expense ratio) are calculated using compound interest:

Gross Value Formula
Gross Value = Initial Investment × (1 + Annual Return %)^Years
Net Value Formula
Net Value = Initial Investment × (1 + Annual Return % − Expense Ratio %)^Years

The total fees paid is the difference between the gross value and the net value.

How to Use This ETF Return Calculator

  1. Enter your initial investment — the amount you plan to invest in the ETF.
  2. Enter the ETF annual return % — the expected average annual return before the expense ratio.
  3. Enter the expense ratio % — found in the ETF's prospectus or on financial data sites.
  4. Enter the number of years — your intended holding period.
  5. Click "Calculate ETF Return" to see the impact of fees on your final value.

Example Scenario

Suppose you invest $10,000 in an ETF with a 9% annual return and a 0.10% expense ratio for 20 years. Your gross value (ignoring fees) would be approximately $56,044. After the 0.10% expense ratio, your net value would be about $55,062 — a difference of $982 in fees. Choosing a fund with a 1.00% expense ratio instead would cost you roughly $8,400 in fees over the same period.

Frequently Asked Questions

An expense ratio is the annual fee charged by an ETF or mutual fund, expressed as a percentage of your investment. It covers management, administrative, and operational costs. A 0.10% expense ratio means you pay $1 per year for every $1,000 invested.

Index ETFs typically have expense ratios between 0.03% and 0.20%. Actively managed ETFs often charge 0.50% to 1.00% or more. Broadly, an expense ratio below 0.20% is considered low for a passively managed fund.

Over long periods, even small expense ratios can significantly reduce your ending balance. A 1% annual fee on a $10,000 investment earning 8% over 30 years costs over $57,000 compared to a 0.05% fee. This is because fees compound just like returns.

Expense ratio is one of the most important factors since it directly reduces your return. However, also consider tracking error, liquidity, and the index being tracked. For similar funds tracking the same index, the lower expense ratio is almost always better.
