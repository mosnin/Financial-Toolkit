Appliance Cost Comparison Formula

Energy Cost Calculation
Daily kWh = Wattage × Hours / 1,000 Annual kWh = Daily kWh × 365 Annual Cost = Annual kWh × Electricity Rate Annual Savings = Old Annual Cost - New Annual Cost Payback Period = Appliance Cost / Annual Savings 10-Year Net Savings = (Annual Savings × 10) - Appliance Cost

For appliances like refrigerators that cycle on and off, the wattage listed is typically the running wattage. Actual average consumption is around 1/3 of rated wattage due to the duty cycle. Adjust hours-per-day to reflect effective run time for cycling appliances.

How to Use This Calculator

  1. Select your appliance type to auto-fill typical old and new wattages, or choose "Custom" to enter manually.
  2. Enter or confirm the wattages -- find your old model's wattage on its label or manufacturer specs.
  3. Enter daily hours of use -- how many hours per day is this appliance actually running?
  4. Enter your electricity rate from your utility bill (typically $0.10-0.30/kWh).
  5. Optionally enter the new appliance cost to calculate payback period.

Example Scenario

Replacing a 1995 Refrigerator with a Modern ENERGY STAR Model

Old model: 800W | New model: 350W | Hours/day: 8 (effective run time) | Rate: $0.16/kWh | New appliance: $800

Old annual kWh: 800W × 8h / 1,000 × 365 = 2,336 kWh

Old annual cost: 2,336 × $0.16 = $373.76/year

New annual kWh: 350W × 8h / 1,000 × 365 = 1,022 kWh

New annual cost: 1,022 × $0.16 = $163.52/year

Annual savings: $210.24/year

Payback: $800 / $210.24 = 3.8 years

10-Year net savings: ($210.24 × 10) - $800 = $1,302.40

Frequently Asked Questions

Wattage is usually on a label on the back or bottom of the appliance, or in the owner's manual. It may be in watts (W) or kilowatts (kW -- multiply by 1,000). For cycling appliances like refrigerators, use the rated wattage and adjust hours down to reflect the duty cycle.

The biggest consumers are: central AC (3,000-5,000W), electric water heater (4,000-5,500W), electric dryer (5,000-6,000W), electric range/oven (2,000-5,000W), and refrigerator (100-400W but runs 24/7). Upgrading aging versions of these offers the greatest savings.

A refrigerator from the 1990s uses 800-1,200 kWh/year. A modern ENERGY STAR model uses 400-600 kWh/year. At $0.16/kWh, that is $64-128/year in savings, or $640-1,280 over 10 years -- often more than the cost of a new refrigerator.

It depends on the efficiency gap and age. If payback is within 5-7 years on an appliance over 10-15 years old, replacement is generally worthwhile -- especially when accounting for growing repair risk. Also check for utility rebates that can significantly reduce the upfront cost.
