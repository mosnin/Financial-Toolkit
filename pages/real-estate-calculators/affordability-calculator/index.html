How the Formula Works

This calculator uses the standard debt-to-income (DTI) ratio method that lenders apply when qualifying borrowers. The maximum total DTI is typically 36% of gross monthly income.

Affordability Calculation
Max Monthly Housing Payment = (Gross Monthly Income x 0.36) - Monthly Debts Max Loan = Reverse of: M = P[r(1+r)^n] / [(1+r)^n - 1] Max Home Price = Max Loan + Down Payment

First, we determine the maximum monthly payment you can afford by taking 36% of your gross monthly income and subtracting your existing debt payments. Then, we reverse the mortgage payment formula to find the maximum loan principal that payment can support. Finally, we add your down payment to get the maximum home price.

Example Scenario

Consider a household earning $90,000 per year ($7,500/month gross) with $400/month in existing debts, a $50,000 down payment, at a 6.5% rate on a 30-year loan. The maximum total debt payment at 36% DTI would be $2,700/month, leaving $2,300/month for the mortgage. Working backward through the mortgage formula, that supports a loan of about $364,000, giving a maximum home price of approximately $414,000.

Frequently Asked Questions

The 28/36 rule is a guideline used by lenders. It states that your monthly housing costs should not exceed 28% of your gross monthly income (front-end ratio), and your total monthly debt payments including housing should not exceed 36% of your gross monthly income (back-end ratio). This calculator uses the 36% back-end ratio to determine your maximum affordable home price.

Your debt-to-income (DTI) ratio is one of the most important factors lenders consider. A lower DTI means more of your income is available for mortgage payments, allowing you to qualify for a larger loan. Most conventional lenders prefer a total DTI of 36% or less, though some loan programs allow up to 43% or even 50%.

This calculator estimates the maximum home price based on your ability to make mortgage principal and interest payments. Your actual affordable price may be lower when factoring in property taxes (typically 0.5-2.5% of home value annually), homeowners insurance, HOA fees, and PMI. Plan for these additional costs to get a complete picture.

The minimum down payment depends on your loan type. Conventional loans typically require 3-5%, FHA loans require 3.5%, and VA and USDA loans may require 0% down. However, putting 20% down avoids private mortgage insurance (PMI) and reduces your monthly payment significantly.

Financial experts generally recommend buying less than your maximum qualification. Just because you can qualify for a certain amount does not mean it is comfortable. Leave room in your budget for savings, emergencies, maintenance, and lifestyle expenses. A good rule of thumb is to aim for a home that costs no more than 3-4 times your annual income.
