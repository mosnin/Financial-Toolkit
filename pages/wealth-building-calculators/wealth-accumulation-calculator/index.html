Wealth Accumulation Formula

Nominal wealth is calculated using the standard future value of annuity formula. Real wealth adjusts for inflation using the Fisher equation:

Nominal Wealth
FV = P(1 + r)n + PMT × [((1 + r)n − 1) / r]
Real Wealth (Inflation-Adjusted)
Real FV = FV / (1 + i)n
Real Return Rate (Fisher Equation)
rreal = ((1 + rnominal) / (1 + i)) − 1

Where P is the starting principal, r is the monthly return rate, PMT is the monthly contribution, n is total months, and i is the annual inflation rate.

How to Use This Calculator

  1. Enter your starting amount — any lump sum you're beginning with.
  2. Enter your monthly contributions — the fixed amount you invest each month.
  3. Enter the annual return rate — your expected portfolio return (7–10% is historically typical for diversified equities).
  4. Enter the number of years — your investment time horizon.
  5. Enter the inflation rate — use 3% as a reasonable long-term estimate.
  6. Click "Calculate Wealth" to see nominal wealth, real wealth, and the contribution vs. gain breakdown.

Example Scenario

Starting with $10,000 and contributing $500 per month at a 7% annual return over 25 years, you would accumulate approximately $456,000 in nominal terms. Adjusted for 3% inflation, that is worth about $221,000 in today's purchasing power. Your total contributions would be $160,000 and investment gains $296,000 — meaning compounding generates nearly twice as much wealth as your direct contributions over this period.

Frequently Asked Questions

Nominal wealth is the dollar figure of your portfolio without adjusting for inflation. Real wealth, or inflation-adjusted wealth, represents what that money is actually worth in today's purchasing power. For example, $500,000 in 20 years at 3% inflation has the purchasing power of about $277,000 today.

The contribution vs. gain ratio shows how much of your final wealth came from money you saved vs. money your money earned. Early on, contributions dominate. Over longer horizons, investment gains overtake contributions — a sign that compounding is doing the heavy lifting for you.

The long-term average US inflation rate is approximately 3% per year. For conservative planning use 3–3.5%; for optimistic projections use 2–2.5%. The Federal Reserve targets 2% inflation over the long run.

Consistently saving $500/month at 7% annual returns over 30 years produces over $600,000. Saving $1,000/month under the same conditions produces over $1.2 million. The exact amount matters less than starting early and staying consistent.
