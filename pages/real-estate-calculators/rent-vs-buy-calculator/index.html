How the Formula Works

This calculator compares the cumulative cost of renting to the cumulative cost of buying over your chosen time period.

Rent vs Buy Comparison
Total Rent Cost = Monthly Rent x 12 x Years Total Buy Cost = Down Payment + Mortgage Payments + Property Taxes Equity Built = Down Payment + Principal Paid Net Buy Cost = Total Buy Cost - Equity Built Breakeven = Year where cumulative Net Buy Cost < cumulative Rent Cost

The rent cost is straightforward: your monthly rent multiplied by the number of months. The buying cost includes the down payment, all mortgage payments, and property taxes. The net buying cost subtracts the equity you build, since that represents retained wealth rather than a pure expense.

How to Use This Calculator

Example Scenario

Suppose you pay $2,000/month in rent and are considering buying a $400,000 home with 20% down ($80,000) at a 6.5% rate with 1.2% property tax. Over 10 years, your total rent cost would be $240,000. The total buying cost including down payment, mortgage payments, and taxes would be approximately $370,000, but you would build roughly $168,000 in equity. The net cost of buying would be about $202,000, making buying the cheaper option after accounting for equity, with a breakeven point around year 5.

In expensive markets with low rent-to-price ratios, renting and investing the difference can sometimes be more financially advantageous, especially for short time horizons.

The breakeven point is the number of years it takes for buying to become cheaper than renting on a cumulative cost basis. Before this point, renting is less expensive. This typically ranges from 3 to 7 years depending on home prices, interest rates, property taxes, and rent levels in your area.

This calculator provides a conservative analysis by not assuming home appreciation or rent increases. In practice, home values historically appreciate around 3-4% annually, and rents tend to rise over time, both of which generally favor buying over longer periods.

The buying costs include the down payment, monthly mortgage payments (principal and interest), and annual property taxes. In reality, homeowners also pay for insurance, maintenance (typically 1-2% of home value per year), HOA fees, and closing costs, which would increase the buying total.

When you buy, a portion of each mortgage payment goes toward building equity (ownership) in the home. This equity acts as forced savings and can be accessed through selling or a home equity loan. Renters build no equity. However, renters can invest their savings elsewhere, so equity is not the only consideration.
