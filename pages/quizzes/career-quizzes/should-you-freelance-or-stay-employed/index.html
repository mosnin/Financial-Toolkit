Should You Freelance or Stay Employed?

Freelancing promises autonomy and income upside — but it also means self-employment tax, no benefits, and the grind of finding clients. Employment offers stability and predictable income but limits your earnings ceiling. This quiz weighs your risk tolerance, client pipeline, financial runway, and current satisfaction to give you a clear, financially-grounded recommendation.

Frequently Asked Questions

As a freelancer you pay self-employment tax (15.3%), provide your own health insurance ($5,000–$15,000/year), and handle retirement savings, unpaid time, and business expenses. A freelancer typically needs to earn 35–50% more than an equivalent salary to net the same take-home. Use the freelance rate calculator to find your break-even rate.

The biggest financial risks are income gaps between clients, underpricing your services (very common), failing to set aside money for self-employment taxes (leading to a surprise bill), and underestimating healthcare costs. Proper rate-setting and a 30% tax savings rate address most of these proactively.

Starting freelance work while still employed is strongly recommended. It lets you build a client base and recurring income without income risk. Once your side freelance income reliably replaces 50–75% of your employment take-home, you have meaningful validation that full-time freelancing is financially viable.
