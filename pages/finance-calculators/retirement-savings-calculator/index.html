Retirement Savings Formula

Compound Interest with Contributions
FV = PV x (1 + r/n)^(n*t) + PMT x [((1 + r/n)^(n*t) - 1) / (r/n)]

Where:
FV = Future Value  |  PV = Present Value (current savings)
r = Annual return rate  |  n = Compounds per year (12)
t = Years until retirement  |  PMT = Monthly contribution

Inflation-Adjusted = FV / (1 + inflation)^t
Monthly Income (4% rule) = FV x 0.04 / 12

This calculator uses the compound interest formula with monthly compounding and regular contributions to project your savings growth. The inflation adjustment shows the purchasing power of your savings in today's dollars.

How to Use This Calculator

  1. Enter your current and retirement ages: The difference determines how many years your money has to grow.
  2. Enter current savings: Include all retirement accounts (401k, IRA, Roth IRA, etc.).
  3. Enter monthly contribution: How much you contribute to retirement each month, including any employer match.
  4. Set return and inflation rates: Use 7% for a moderate stock portfolio. Historical inflation averages about 3%.
  5. Review results: The projected amount, inflation-adjusted value, and estimated monthly retirement income based on the 4% withdrawal rule.

Example Scenario

Maria is 30 years old and plans to retire at 65. She has $50,000 saved and contributes $500/month. Assuming 7% annual returns and 3% inflation:

  • Years to retirement: 35 years
  • Projected savings: Approximately $1,148,000
  • Inflation-adjusted value: Approximately $408,000 (in today's dollars)
  • Monthly retirement income (4% rule): Approximately $3,827/month ($1,360 in today's dollars)

This illustrates why starting early and contributing consistently makes such a significant difference.

Frequently Asked Questions

A common rule of thumb is to save 25 times your annual expenses (the 4% rule). For example, if you need $50,000 per year, aim for $1.25 million. The right amount depends on your lifestyle, healthcare needs, and other income sources.

Historically, diversified stock portfolios have returned about 7-10% per year before inflation. A conservative estimate of 6-7% is often used for long-term projections. Bond-heavy portfolios might assume 4-5%.

Inflation reduces the purchasing power of your money over time. Adjusting for inflation gives you a realistic picture of what your savings will actually be worth when you retire.

The sooner the better. Starting early gives your money more time to compound. Even small contributions in your 20s can grow significantly. Delaying by just 10 years can cut your final savings in half.

The 4% rule suggests you can withdraw 4% of your retirement savings each year without running out over a 30-year retirement. With $1 million saved, you could withdraw $40,000 per year.
