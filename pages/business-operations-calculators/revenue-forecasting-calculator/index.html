Revenue Forecasting Formulas

Each month's MRR is derived by applying gross new growth and subtracting churn from the prior month's base.

New MRR Added
New MRR = Previous MRR x Monthly Growth Rate
Churned MRR
Churned MRR = Previous MRR x Monthly Churn Rate
Ending MRR
Ending MRR = Previous MRR + New MRR - Churned MRR
ARR
ARR = Month 12 MRR x 12
Net New MRR (Total)
Net New MRR = Month 12 MRR - Starting MRR

How to Use This Calculator

  1. Enter your current MRR -- your total monthly recurring revenue right now.
  2. Enter monthly growth rate -- the percentage of new MRR you expect to add each month from new customers and expansions.
  3. Enter monthly churn rate -- the percentage of existing MRR lost each month to cancellations.
  4. Optionally enter a one-time revenue boost -- a projected lump-sum revenue event that will be reflected in Month 6 of the forecast.
  5. Click "Generate Revenue Forecast" to see the full 12-month table.

Example Scenario

A SaaS company has $80,000 MRR, a 7% monthly growth rate, and 2% monthly churn. No one-time boost.

  • Month 1 New MRR: $80,000 x 7% = $5,600
  • Month 1 Churn: $80,000 x 2% = $1,600
  • Month 1 Ending MRR: $80,000 + $5,600 - $1,600 = $84,000
  • Month 12 MRR (estimated): ~$163,000
  • ARR Projection: ~$1.96M

Net growth of 5% per month (7% new minus 2% churn) roughly doubles MRR in 12 months and sets the business on track toward $2M ARR.

Frequently Asked Questions

MRR (Monthly Recurring Revenue) is the predictable monthly revenue a subscription business earns from its active customers. It excludes one-time fees and is the core metric for SaaS and subscription business health.

ARR (Annual Recurring Revenue) is the annualized version of MRR, calculated as MRR x 12. It gives a yearly revenue baseline and is widely used for benchmarking subscription business scale.

Net new MRR is the change in MRR during a period, accounting for new revenue added minus MRR lost to churn. Positive net new MRR means the business is growing its recurring revenue base.

Churn reduces the existing revenue base each month. Even with positive new customer growth, high churn can offset gains and suppress net new MRR. Accurate revenue forecasting requires accounting for churn alongside new growth.
