How Budget Percentages Are Calculated

Formula
Category % = (Category Spending / Gross Income) × 100
Surplus / Deficit = Gross Income − Total Spending
50/30/20 Needs Target = Gross Income × 0.50

Each category percentage tells you exactly how much of every dollar you earn is consumed by that expense. Comparing your real percentages to the 50/30/20 benchmark reveals whether you are over-spending in needs, under-saving, or have room to increase wants.

How to Use This Calculator

  1. Enter your monthly gross income: Use your pre-tax income for standard benchmark comparisons.
  2. Fill in each spending category: Leave fields blank or at zero for categories that do not apply to you.
  3. Click "Calculate Percentages": Instantly see each category as a percent of income.
  4. Review the 50/30/20 comparison: The color-coded table shows whether each bucket is on target, over, or under the recommended guideline.
  5. Identify your surplus or deficit: Use this number to plan debt payoff, savings increases, or spending cuts.

Example Scenario

Jordan earns $6,000/month gross and spends: Housing $1,600, Food $550, Transport $350, Healthcare $150, Entertainment $400, Savings $700, Other $250. Total spending is $4,000 — a $2,000 surplus. Housing is 26.7%, well within the 30% guideline. Entertainment at 6.7% leaves room for discretionary spending. Savings at 11.7% is below the recommended 20%, suggesting Jordan could redirect some of that surplus to savings.

Frequently Asked Questions

Most financial experts recommend spending no more than 28-30% of your gross monthly income on housing costs. Under the 50/30/20 rule, housing falls within the 50% needs bucket, shared with utilities, groceries, and insurance.

The 50/30/20 rule allocates 50% of after-tax income to needs, 30% to wants, and 20% to savings and debt repayment. It is a simple guideline popularized by Senator Elizabeth Warren that works for most income levels.

For standard benchmark comparisons most guidelines reference gross income. For practical day-to-day spending decisions, your net take-home pay is the most relevant figure since that is the money actually available to spend.

A surplus means total spending is less than income — the remainder can go toward savings or investments. A deficit means you are spending more than you earn, which requires either reducing expenses or finding ways to increase income.
