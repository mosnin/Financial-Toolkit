Profit Forecast Formulas

Each month's projections are calculated using compound growth applied to a consistent cost structure.

Monthly Revenue (Month N)
Revenue(N) = Starting Revenue x (1 + Monthly Growth Rate)^N
Gross Profit
Gross Profit = Revenue x (1 - COGS%)
Net Profit
Net Profit = Gross Profit - Fixed Operating Expenses

How to Use This Calculator

  1. Enter current monthly revenue -- your most recent month's revenue as the starting point.
  2. Enter monthly growth rate -- your expected month-over-month revenue growth (e.g., 5 for 5%). Use negative values for declining revenue.
  3. Enter COGS % -- the percentage of revenue consumed by direct production costs (e.g., 40 means COGS is 40% of revenue each month).
  4. Enter fixed monthly operating expenses -- costs that remain relatively stable regardless of revenue.
  5. Enter months to forecast -- choose 1 to 24 months ahead.
  6. Click "Generate Forecast" to see a full month-by-month table.

Example Scenario

A startup has $50,000 current MRR, a 5% monthly growth rate, 40% COGS, and $20,000 fixed monthly expenses. Looking ahead 12 months:

  • Month 1 Revenue: $50,000 x 1.05 = $52,500
  • Month 12 Revenue: $50,000 x (1.05)^12 = ~$89,542
  • Month 12 Gross Profit: $89,542 x 60% = $53,725
  • Month 12 Net Profit: $53,725 - $20,000 = $33,725

At 5% monthly growth, the business roughly doubles revenue in 12 months and generates increasingly strong profits as fixed costs become a smaller proportion of revenue.

Frequently Asked Questions

To forecast profit, start with current monthly revenue, apply a monthly growth rate to project future revenue, then subtract estimated COGS and fixed operating expenses to arrive at projected profit for each month.

COGS percentage is the ratio of cost of goods sold to revenue. For example, a 40% COGS means that for every $100 in revenue, $40 goes toward direct production costs, leaving 60% as gross margin.

Profit forecasts are estimates based on assumptions. Market changes, unexpected costs, and fluctuating growth rates can cause actual results to differ. Update forecasts regularly as new data becomes available.

Revenue forecasting projects future sales. Profit forecasting goes further by subtracting estimated costs from projected revenue to show expected profitability. Profit forecasting requires assumptions about cost structure in addition to growth.
