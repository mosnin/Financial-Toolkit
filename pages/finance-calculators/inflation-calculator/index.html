Inflation Formula

Formulas
Future Cost = Current Amount x (1 + Inflation Rate)^Years

Purchasing Power = Current Amount / (1 + Inflation Rate)^Years

Total Inflation Effect = Future Cost - Current Amount

The future cost shows what something that costs a certain amount today will cost in the future. The purchasing power shows how much your current money will be worth in future dollars. The total inflation effect is the dollar amount of the price increase.

How to Use This Calculator

  1. Enter the current amount: This can be the price of a good, your salary, or any dollar amount you want to project forward.
  2. Set the inflation rate: The US historical average is about 3%. You can use current rates or any projection.
  3. Enter the number of years: How far into the future you want to project.
  4. Click "Calculate": See the future cost, your reduced purchasing power, and the total inflation effect.

Example Scenario

Suppose you want to know what a basket of groceries costing $200 today will cost in 20 years at 3% annual inflation:

  • Current cost: $200
  • Future cost: $200 x (1.03)^20 = $361.22
  • Purchasing power of $200: $200 / (1.03)^20 = $110.73 (in today's terms, your $200 will only buy $110.73 worth of goods)
  • Total inflation effect: $361.22 - $200 = $161.22

This means the same groceries will cost 80.6% more in 20 years.

Frequently Asked Questions

Inflation is the rate at which the general level of prices for goods and services rises over time, causing purchasing power to decrease. When inflation goes up, every dollar you have buys fewer goods and services.

In the United States, the long-term average annual inflation rate is approximately 3%. However, inflation can vary significantly from year to year.

If your savings earn less interest than the inflation rate, your money loses purchasing power over time. At 3% inflation, $100 today will only buy about $74 worth of goods in 10 years.

Inflation can be caused by increased demand for goods and services (demand-pull), rising costs of production (cost-push), or expansion of the money supply. Central banks use monetary policy to keep inflation at moderate levels.
