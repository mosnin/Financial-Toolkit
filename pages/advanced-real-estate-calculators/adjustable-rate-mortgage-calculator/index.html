ARM Payment Formula

ARM payments use the standard amortization formula at the current rate:

Monthly Payment
M = P [ r(1+r)^n ] / [ (1+r)^n - 1 ]

Where P is the loan balance, r is the current monthly rate, and n is remaining payments. The worst-case payment uses the initial rate plus the lifetime cap.

How to Use This Calculator

  1. Enter the loan amount - the total mortgage amount.
  2. Enter the initial ARM rate - the starting interest rate for the fixed period.
  3. Enter the adjustment period - how many years before the rate first adjusts.
  4. Enter the lifetime rate cap - the maximum the rate can increase over the loan life.
  5. Enter a comparable fixed rate - for side-by-side comparison.
  6. Click "Calculate ARM" to compare payments.

Example Scenario

A $300,000 loan with a 5/1 ARM at 5.0% initial rate and a 5% lifetime cap would have an initial monthly payment of about $1,610. If the rate hits the cap at 10.0%, the worst-case payment rises to approximately $2,633. A comparable 6.5% fixed-rate mortgage would cost $1,896/month, meaning the ARM saves $286/month initially but risks costing $737/month more at the cap.

Frequently Asked Questions

An ARM has an interest rate that changes periodically after an initial fixed-rate period. Common structures include 5/1, 7/1, and 10/1 ARMs where the first number is the fixed period in years.

Rate caps limit how much your interest rate can increase per adjustment period and over the life of the loan. A typical cap structure is 2/2/5, meaning 2% per adjustment, 2% per year, and 5% lifetime cap.

ARMs can be beneficial if you plan to sell or refinance before the fixed period ends, if rates are expected to drop, or if the initial rate savings are significant enough to justify the risk.

The index rate is a benchmark rate (like SOFR or Treasury rate) that the lender uses plus a margin to determine your adjusted rate. When the index rises, your rate rises; when it falls, your rate may decrease.
